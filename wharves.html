The Wharves

"On a nearer approach, the city makes a poor appearance, as nothing is visible from the water but confused heaps of wooden storehouses, crowded upon each other, the chief of which are built upon platforms of artificial ground, and wharfs which project a considerable way into the river. The wharfs are of a rectangular form, and built of wood; they jut out in every direction, and are well adapted for the accomodation of shipping, the largest merchant vessels being able to lie close alongside them. Behind these wharfs, and parallel to the river, runs Water-Street."

-- from Isaac Weld, Travels Through the States of North America...During the Years 1795, 1796 and 1797, p.3.

Early Philadelphia, being a port, aligned itself along the Delaware River. That is, people clustered as close as they could to the wharves for both commerce and residence, time being money then as now. Quite a few people did business and lived in structures on or next to the docks, and John MacPherson chose to denominate these addresses as "River-side." Francis White, on the other hand, with his more practical bent, decided to follow common practice by stating whatever wharf the person in question was on-- "Market Street wharf," for example. The following list is White's listings, arranged as they would have appeared on a map on which north is "up". No attempt has yet been made to align these listings.
