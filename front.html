(1785) Waln, Robert, merchant, Front between Arch and Race
A marvelous mahogany tambour desk (a desk which closes like a roll-top desk, but which closes in a simple arc rather than in an S-curve), made for merchant Robert Waln, is in the Philadelphia Museum of Art, and is illustrated and discussed in Beatrice Garvan's Federal Philadelphia: The Athens of the Western World, pp.84-85. This desk is attributed to Daniel Trotter (d.1800), who worked on Water Street above Arch, but who was also listed in Elfreth's Alley-- possibly one was his workshop and one his home-- and possibly also his apprentice (and later, partner) Ephraim Haines (1775-1837). This desk is remarkable for several reasons, but most of all for the script W executed in silver on each side and the silver candle-arms mounted to the top on each side.
According to Garvan, in the years between 1785 and 1800, he married, built a new house on 2nd Street which was wallpapered and furnished from all the best shops, and ordered a new carriage from David Clark (6th between Market and Chestnut).

Gilbert Gaw, windsor chair maker, lived on this side of this block in the 1790s; according to his advertisements, he was at #90 N. Front St., twelve doors above Mulberry or Arch St.

NOTES

According to the 1909 "Publication No. 5" of the City Historical Society of Philadelphia, "At No. 918 South Front Street is what appears to be half a house. It is very old, doubtlessly of Swedish origin, and in consequence we do not know the reason for its peculiar style of architecture. It might be supposed to be accidental, but for the fact that at 614 South Front Street stands another, and probably these two are not the only 'half-houses' in the neighborhood." The author, M. Antonia Lynch, was wrong about such houses being of Swedish origin; gambrel-roof houses built in halves like that were once relatively common in other, non-Swedish areas of the city, too.

Sparks' shot-making establishment was on Front Street. Of this only the well-known tower remains. Publication No. 5 notes that there was an old cemetery behind the tower, and that when the publication was written in the early 20th century only three headstones were at all visible. They were dug out and proved to be the headstones of Elizabeth Snell, wife of Captain James Snell, who died at age 40 on April 29, 1798; Elizabeth McKechnie, wife of Lundin McKechnie, who died May 24, 1797, and Elizabeth Glensey, daughter of Richard and Isabelle Glensey, who died in 1800 at the age of 86. Ms. Lynch, however, did not say what, if anything, was done with these headstones or graves.

The same publication remarked that the home of early U.S. naval hero Stephen Decatur was at #611 S. Front Street (probably the modern numbering).

(same) "784 South Front Street, bearing the date of 1763 on the wall, was the residence of George Ord, who was born in this house, and died here January 24, 1866, at the age of eighty-four. He was President of the Academy of Natural Sciences, and an active member of the American Philosophical Society and other scientific and literary associations.

(same) "The Weccacoe Fire Company, founded May 1, 1800, was located first at the corner of Front and Christian Streets; next at the corner of Union Alley and Front Street; then at the Prime Street landing, and finally in 1817 was granted permission by the Commissioners to use the lot in front of their hall, on Second Street."

According to Rum Punch and Revolution by Peter Thompson, the Blue Anchor tavern stood in the middle of Front Street and had to be demolished to allow the street to go through. (p.22) The work did not nail down where on Front Street it stood, however.

(same source) Samuel Carpenter (brother of Joshua Carpenter who built Tun Tavern) built two taverns on the east side of Front Street: the Globe Tavern and Carpenter's Coffeehouse. (p.28) Hannah Hubbard was the tenant of the Globe in 1715-1716, (p.64) and one Grimstone Boude was its tenant for almost 20 years, until his death. (p.31)

(same) The Pennypot Tavern, one of two standing already when Philadelphia was founded, stood at Pegg's Run (a creek), near Front Street just north of Vine. Shipbuilder James West arrived in Philadelphia in 1684 and bought the Pennypot in 1689. (p.28-29)

(same) Alice Guest (d.1705) who established the Crooked Billet Tavern, owned a house by the time of her death on the west side of Front Street. (p.31)
